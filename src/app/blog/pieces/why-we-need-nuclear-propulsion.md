Strapping nuclear power to a rocket is terrifying for some people. What if it ‘goes off’ over our heads? What if it rains Uranium? Here is why we need nuclear propulsion. 

Nuclear propulsion is not (mostly) a nuclear bomb-based engine. Conventionally, nuclear propulsion is simply electric propulsion without solar panels. Ionic thrusters shoot out heavy gases, like krypton ions, at immense speeds using electric fields. This electric field is made from power from solar panels. The only difference against any other satellite is that solar is replaced by radioactive isotopes that slowly release energy over 20-30 years. 

### It's not going to explode.

<img src="/Media/blog/explosion.jpg" alt="explosion" />

Some non-scientific people believe that if the rocket blows up, it will become a giant nuclear bomb. It won’t. Firstly, radioactive isotopes are not the same as radioactive metals used in bombs. Secondly, if countries spend decades trying to build nuclear weapons, one crude explosion is not going to be able to enrich the uranium and then blow it up. 

Worst-case scenario, the payload explodes, and it will rain Uranium, but…. 

### It won't rain uranium.

Uranium in itself is not used, and the isotopes used release energy so slowly that it will only be harmful if you scavenge the crash site, find a pellet, and sleep with it in your pillow. 

Also, rockets don't explode that much anymore(Kinda. Don’t google “New Glenn”, Please.). Even when they do, engineers usually take simple precautions. Such radioactive fuel cells are usually wrapped in special casings that prevent the uranium from leaching into the ground if the vehicle is lost in a fiery ball of plasma, or a “Rapid Unscheduled Disassembly”.
